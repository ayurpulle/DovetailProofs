\title{What Every Exact Predictor Must Compute:\\ Divisibility Lower Bounds from Predictive Monoids}
\author{Ayur Pulle\\ \small{Dovetail Research}}
\date{\small August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
Every exact predictor of a stochastic process contains, as a quotient
of its update dynamics, the process's canonical predictive machine.
We prove this at the level of transition monoids and ask which
properties of the canonical monoid $S_p$ are thereby forced on every
predictor.  The canonical monoid is the syntactic monoid of the map
from histories to predictive states, so computing predictive states
and multiplying in $S_p$ are the same task.  For predictors with
finitely many states, every subgroup of $S_p$ is the image of a
subgroup of the predictor's update monoid, and aperiodicity and
Krohn--Rhodes group complexity are bounded accordingly, with the
bounds attained.  A predictor that stores its whole history shows
that finiteness cannot be dropped.  For predictors with no recurrent
state the constraint moves to the function computed: exact
next-symbol prediction lies in $\mathrm{AC}^0$ when $S_p$ is
aperiodic and is $\mathrm{NC}^1$-complete when $S_p$ contains a
non-solvable group, so no constant-depth transformer of logarithmic
precision predicts such a process at all lengths unless
$\mathrm{TC}^0=\mathrm{NC}^1$.  A word by word companion bounds
forced memory, and a subgroup of $S_p$ forces as many live states as
its smallest faithful permutation action.  All of these invariants
read $S_p$ alone.  They are blind to transition likelihoods, which we
isolate in an extension of $S_p$ containing the minimal linear
predictor, and for two standard example processes we compute that
$S_p$ is determined by the pattern of possible words alone.  The
lattice of congruences of $S_p$, the invariant this work began from,
is also inherited, as an interval, but it is non-distributive already
for first order Markov chains predicted by storing the last symbol,
and certifies nothing about internal representations.  We then test
what training finds.  Small transformers trained to predict processes
chosen by the class of $S_p$ keep predicting beyond the trained
length when $S_p$ is aperiodic, including for a process with no
synchronising word, and stop when $S_p$ is a group; solvable and
non-solvable groups are not distinguishable.  Linear readouts of
their activations recover the predictive state and not the element of
$S_p$ of a stretch of input.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:positioning}

Let a world produce a sequence of observations from a finite alphabet
$\Obs$.  An exact predictive agent has enough information to return
the conditional distribution of every finite future, it may
nevertheless have redundant states or implementation specificities.
Our question is: which predictive structure, the canonical
predictive states together with their observation labelled updates,
must every such agent contain, or realise as a quotient?

The starting point is a quotient theorem.  Every reachable exact
predictor maps onto the process's canonical predictive machine, and a
descent lemma turns that map of states into a surjection of
transition monoids $\Phi:S_A\to S_p$ (Section~\ref{sec:agent}).  The
canonical monoid $S_p$ is therefore a quotient of the update monoid
of every exact predictor, and the paper asks which of its properties
are forced on the predictor as a result.

The answer has four parts.  First, $S_p$ is the syntactic monoid of
the map sending a history to its predictive state, so computing
predictive states and multiplying in $S_p$ are two forms of one task
(Section~\ref{sec:syntactic}).  Second, for a predictor with finitely
many states, every subgroup of $S_p$ is the image of a subgroup of
$S_A$, and aperiodicity and group complexity are bounded by those of
$S_p$, with equality attained by the canonical machine.  A predictor
that stores its entire history has a free update monoid, so the
finiteness hypothesis is necessary.  Third, for exactly such
predictors, a transformer reading the whole history being the
intended case, the constraint is on the function computed: exact
prediction has the circuit complexity of the word problem of $S_p$,
which is governed by the groups $S_p$ contains
(Section~\ref{subsec:parallel}).  Fourth, a word by word bound: the
number of live states any exact predictor holds after a word $w$ is
at least the live rank of $\tau_w$, and a subgroup of $S_p$ forces at
least as many as its smallest faithful permutation action
(Section~\ref{sec:rank}).

These invariants all read the monoid $S_p$ and nothing else.  Two
further layers sit around it.  Below is a possibility machine
recording only which futures can occur (Section~\ref{sec:support}),
above, the minimal linear predictor embeds into an extension of $S_p$
by transition likelihoods, and passing back to $S_p$ is exactly the
projection that discards the likelihood coordinate
(Sections~\ref{sec:linear}, \ref{sec:quantitative}).  The layers
separate in examples, which bounds what any invariant of $S_p$ can
say.

The invariant this work began from is the lattice of congruences of
$S_p$.  It is inherited by every exact predictor as an interval, and
the non-distributive configurations it can carry cannot be removed by
any reimplementation.  But they are generic: a first order Markov
chain whose exact predictor stores only the last symbol already
carries an $M_3$, while every invariant of the previous paragraphs is
trivial on it.  Section~\ref{sec:congruence} records the inheritance
and its limits.

All of this concerns prediction, not control.
Section~\ref{sec:controllers} states the one hypothesis under which a
controller inherits the same structure and why optimality does not
supply it.  No claim is made that a neural network's raw hidden state
is automatically a linear realisation, or that any of this locates
features in activations.

Whether trained networks represent the elements of $S_p$, or only
their effect on the current state, and whether the class of $S_p$
says anything about what training finds, are empirical questions.
Section~\ref{sec:experiment} reports an experiment on both, with
small transformers trained on short histories of processes chosen by
their monoid.  Three things come out.  The models keep predicting
beyond the trained length when $S_p$ is aperiodic and stop when $S_p$
is a group, and this is aperiodicity and not the presence of a reset
word, since a process that is aperiodic with no synchronising word
behaves like the other aperiodic ones.  The line between solvable
and non-solvable groups, which is where the complexity theorem puts
its conditional impossibility, is not visible: $A_5$ is no harder
than $S_3$, $S_4$ or $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3$.  And the
networks hold the predictive state and almost nothing else; a linear
readout finds no more of the element $\tau_w$ than the states and the
literal symbols already give.  So the dichotomy that training
respects is the coarsest one in Table~\ref{tab:classes}, between
$\mathrm{AC}^0$ and everything above it, and $S_p$ is present in
these networks as the monoid of the update they compute and not as a
represented quantity.

The state level foundations are classical: identification of histories
by their conditional future laws, with any equally predictive rival
refining that partition
\cite{CrutchfieldYoung1989,ShaliziCrutchfield2001}, the
$\varepsilon$-transducer for action/output processes
\cite{BarnettCrutchfield2015}, and Hankel minimisation
with its quotient onto the minimal realisation
\cite{Fliess1974,Jaeger2000}.  The algebraic tools are classical as
well: syntactic monoids and the star-free theorem
\cite{Pin1986,Schutzenberger1965}, the Krohn--Rhodes decomposition
and group complexity \cite{KrohnRhodes1965,RhodesSteinberg2009}, and
the circuit complexity of word problems
\cite{BarringtonTherien1988,Barrington1989,BarringtonEtAl1992}.
Their use to bound transformers is established for automata and
formal languages given in advance
\cite{MerrillSabharwal2023,LiuEtAl2023,YangChiangAngluin2024}.  What
is added here is the passage from a stochastic process to the monoid
those results apply to, and the proof that this monoid is forced on
every exact predictor.  Two lines are engaged rather than built on:
behavioural recovery of an environment from goal conditioned policies
\cite{Richens2025,Cifuentes2026,LuEtAl2026}, taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:controllers}, and the empirical study of predictive
structure in trained networks \cite{ShaiEtAl2024}, whose example
processes are computed exactly in
Section~\ref{subsec:computed-support}.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item a single descent lemma that converts any update commuting
        surjection of state machines into a monoid surjection, making
        $S_p$ a quotient of every exact predictor's update monoid
        (Lemma~\ref{lem:descent}, Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient}),
  \item the identification of $S_p$ and of the possibility monoid as
        syntactic monoids (Lemma~\ref{lem:syntactic}),
  \item the transport of subgroups, aperiodicity and group complexity
        to every finite exact predictor, attained by the canonical
        machine, with an unbounded-memory predictor showing
        finiteness is needed (Theorem~\ref{thm:transport},
        Example~\ref{ex:free-predictor}),
  \item for predictors with no recurrent state, a two-way reduction
        between exact prediction and the word problem of $S_p$,
        placing prediction in a circuit class determined by the
        groups in $S_p$, with a conditional impossibility result for
        constant-depth transformers
        (Theorem~\ref{thm:pred-complexity},
        Corollary~\ref{cor:transformers}),
  \item a word by word memory bound: the live rank profile of the
        process lower bounds the distinctions any exact predictor
        retains after each word, and subgroups of $S_p$ sit at rank
        levels that bound them (Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-bound},
        Proposition~\ref{prop:group-rank}),
  \item the layers around $S_p$: a possibility quotient below and a
        likelihood extension above, with exact computations placing
        two standard example processes entirely at the possibility
        layer, generic Markov chains entirely at the likelihood
        layer, and one process strictly between
        (Sections~\ref{sec:support}, \ref{sec:quantitative}),
  \item a scope limit on the congruence lattice: its inherited
        interval is non-distributive generically and is not a
        certificate of entangled features
        (Section~\ref{sec:congruence}),
  \item an experiment on trained transformers, with processes built
        to have a prescribed monoid: length generalisation follows
        aperiodicity of $S_p$ and not synchronisation or solvability,
        and the activations encode states and not monoid elements
        (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}).
\end{enumerate}
All code supporting the computations, exact and reproducible, is
available in the paper's repository \cite{DovetailRepo}.

\section{Stochastic languages and conditional futures}
\label{sec:process}

\begin{definition}[Stochastic language]
A stochastic language over a finite alphabet $\Obs$ is a function
\[
  p:\Obs^*\longrightarrow[0,1]
\]
such that
\[
  p(\eps)=1,
  \qquad
  p(u)=\sum_{o\in\Obs}p(uo)
  \quad (u\in\Obs^*).
\]
Here $p(u)$ is the probability that the process begins with the word $u$,
the second condition is simple consistency, that the summed probabilities of all one symbol extensions to $u$ are
equal to the probability of $u$.
\end{definition}

If the process is also stationary,
then its probabilities additionally satisfy
\[
  p(u)=\sum_{o\in\Obs}p(ou).
\]

Stationarity may be imposed, but none of the quotient arguments uses it.

For a history $u$ with $p(u)>0$, define the conditional future
\[
  q_u(v)=\frac{p(uv)}{p(u)}
  \qquad(v\in\Obs^*).
\]
Then $q_u(\eps)=1$ and $q_u(v)=\sum_o q_u(vo)$.  Histories $u,u'$ are
predictively equivalent when $q_u=q_{u'}$.

If $p(u)=0$, consistency and non negativity imply $p(uv)=0$ for every
$v$, and then conditional prediction after $u$ is undefined.  Represent
every such impossible continuation by one symbol
$\dagger$.

\begin{definition}[Canonical predictive states]
The predictive state set of $p$ is
\[
  X_p=\{q_u:p(u)>0\}.
\]
Define $\widehat X_p=X_p\cup\{\dagger\}$.
\end{definition}

This is the usual predictive/causal state identification by complete future
law \cite{CrutchfieldYoung1989,ShaliziCrutchfield2001}, with one
deviation to flag: we condition on finite histories, so $X_p$ can
contain transient states, where computational mechanics classes almost
every infinite past.  The relation between the two presentations, and
their generator counterpart, is settled in the finite-state ergodic
case in \cite{TraversCrutchfield2025}.

For $o\in\Obs$, define $\tau_o:\widehat X_p\to\widehat X_p$ by
\[
  \tau_o(q_u)=
  \begin{cases}
    q_{uo},&p(uo)>0,\\
    \dagger,&p(uo)=0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \tau_o(\dagger)=\dagger.
\]

\begin{lemma}
The map $\tau_o$ is well-defined: if $q_u=q_{u'}$, then either both
$p(uo)$ and $p(u'o)$ vanish, or $q_{uo}=q_{u'o}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The one symbol probabilities agree:
\[
  \frac{p(uo)}{p(u)}=q_u(o)=q_{u'}(o)
  =\frac{p(u'o)}{p(u')}.
\]
Thus one is zero exactly when the other is.  If they are positive, then for every
$v$,
\[
  q_{uo}(v)=\frac{q_u(ov)}{q_u(o)}
  =\frac{q_{u'}(ov)}{q_{u'}(o)}
  =q_{u'o}(v).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let
\[
  S_p=\langle\tau_o:o\in\Obs\rangle
  \subseteq\mathcal T_{\widehat X_p}
\]
be the transformation monoid generated by the predictive updates, inside
$\mathcal T_{\widehat X_p}$, the monoid of all maps from $\widehat X_p$ to
itself under composition.  Composition is
ordinary function composition.  For a word $w=o_1\cdots o_n$, write
$\tau_w=\tau_{o_n}\circ\cdots\circ\tau_{o_1}$, so that
$\tau_w(q_u)=q_{uw}$ whenever $p(uw)>0$.
\end{definition}

For later use, write
\[
  L_p=\{w\in\Obs^*:p(w)>0\}
\]
for the set of possible words.  Consistency gives
$p(u)\ge p(uv)$, so $L_p$ is closed under prefixes, and
$p(u)=\sum_o p(uo)$ shows every possible word has at least one possible
one symbol extension.

\section{Every exact predictive agent contains the canonical machine}
\label{sec:agent}

The results of this paper repeatedly use one mechanism, so we
state it here.

\begin{lemma}[Descent]
\label{lem:descent}
Let $Q$ and $Y$ be sets, each equipped with a family of maps
$\delta_o:Q\to Q$ and $\tau_o:Y\to Y$ indexed by the same set
$\Obs$, and let $L:Q\to Y$ be a surjection satisfying
\[
  L\circ\delta_o=\tau_o\circ L
  \qquad(o\in\Obs).
\]
Write $S_Q=\langle\delta_o\rangle$ and $S_Y=\langle\tau_o\rangle$ for the
generated transformation monoids, with the composition convention
$\delta_w=\delta_{o_n}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{o_1}$ for
$w=o_1\cdots o_n$, and likewise for $\tau_w$.  Then
\[
  \Phi(\delta_w)=\tau_w
\]
is a well-defined surjective monoid homomorphism $\Phi:S_Q\to S_Y$.
In particular $S_Y$ is a quotient of $S_Q$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Iterating the displayed equations gives $L\circ\delta_w=\tau_w\circ L$ for
every word $w$.  If $\delta_w=\delta_v$ then
$\tau_w\circ L=\tau_v\circ L$, and surjectivity of $L$ forces
$\tau_w=\tau_v$, so $\Phi$ is well-defined.  For words $v,w$ the convention
gives $\delta_w\circ\delta_v=\delta_{vw}$ and
$\tau_w\circ\tau_v=\tau_{vw}$, so $\Phi$ respects composition, and it hits
every generator $\tau_o$, so it is surjective.
\end{proof}

No structure on $Q$ or $Y$ is assumed: no dead state, no linearity, no
probabilistic interpretation.  Nor does the proof use that $\Obs$ is
finite.  Everything specific to prediction enters
through the construction of a particular surjection $L$.  The lemma
turns a statement about states into one about monoids, and from there
the question of this paper is which properties of a monoid pass to
its quotients, or more generally to its divisors, a monoid $T$
\emph{divides} $S$ when $T$ is a quotient of a submonoid of $S$.
Sections~\ref{sec:syntactic} and~\ref{sec:rank} transport the
informative ones.  Section~\ref{sec:congruence} treats the most
obvious one, the lattice of congruences, and shows why it is not
enough.  One convention remark for later use: a monoid and its
opposite (the same elements multiplied in reverse order) have the
same congruences, the same subgroups up to isomorphism, and the same
idempotents, so composition-order conventions never affect the
invariants used below.



\begin{definition}
A reachable exact predictive agent for $p$ consists of
\[
  A=(Q,q_0,d,\{\delta_o\}_{o\in\Obs},F),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $Q$ is the set of agent states,
  \item $q_0\in Q$ is the initial state,
  \item $d\in Q$ is the dead state, the agent's version of
        $\dagger$, used for impossible continuations, like 
        $\dagger$, a single formal element adjoined to the live states,
  \item each $\delta_o:Q\to Q$ is the state update on observing $o$,
  \item $F$ reads out the complete conditional future law
        represented by a live state, its domain $Q^+$ is
        defined below.
\end{itemize}


For $u=o_1\cdots o_n$, write
\[
  \delta_u=\delta_{o_n}\circ\cdots\circ\delta_{o_1},
  \qquad \delta_\eps=\id.
\]
The possible reachable states are
\[
  Q^+=\{\delta_u(q_0):p(u)>0\},
\]
the dead state $d$ is not in $Q^+$, and
$Q=Q^+\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{d\}$.  Each $\delta_o:Q\to Q$ satisfies
$\delta_o(d)=d$ and
\[
  p(u)>0,\ p(uo)=0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \delta_o(\delta_u(q_0))=d.
\]
Finally, $F:Q^+\to X_p$ returns the state's complete conditional future law.
The predictor is exact when, for every $u$ with $p(u)>0$,
\[
  F(\delta_u(q_0))=q_u.
\]
\end{definition}

After an impossible extension there is no conditional future law, 
so exactness constrains nothing, rather than leave the update maps undefined, we route every impossible extension to the 
single dead state and keep it there. 
We call this the trimming convention, all dead branches are sent to one point, 
so the update maps are total and any two exact predictors can differ only on states that matter.


\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:predictor-quotient}
Let $A$ be a reachable exact predictive agent for $p$.  There is a surjection
\[
  L:Q\rightarrow\widehat X_p
\]
such that
\[
  L\circ\delta_o=\tau_o\circ L
  \qquad(o\in\Obs).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a non-dead reachable state $q=\delta_u(q_0)$, set $L(q)=q_u$, and send the
agent's dead state to $\dagger$.  This is well-defined: if
$\delta_u(q_0)=\delta_v(q_0)$, exactness gives
$q_u=F(\delta_u(q_0))=F(\delta_v(q_0))=q_v$.

Every element $q_u\in X_p$ is hit by the state $\delta_u(q_0)$, so $L$ is
surjective.  If $p(uo)>0$, then
\[
  L(\delta_o\delta_u(q_0))=q_{uo}
  =\tau_o(q_u)=\tau_oL(\delta_u(q_0)).
\]
If $p(uo)=0$, both sides are $\dagger$ by the trimming convention.  The equality
also holds at the absorbing dead state.
\end{proof}

This is an analogue of
the Refinement Lemma from computational mechanics, any equally predictive rival partition refines the
causal state partition, up to null histories \cite{ShaliziCrutchfield2001}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} lifts that state-level refinement to transition
monoids.

Let $S_A=\langle\delta_o:o\in\Obs\rangle\subseteq\mathcal T_Q$.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:quotient}
Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} to the surjection of
Theorem~\ref{thm:predictor-quotient} yields a surjective monoid
homomorphism
\[
  \Phi:S_A\rightarrow S_p,
  \qquad
  \Phi(\delta_w)=\tau_w.
\]
So $S_p$ is a quotient, and in particular a divisor, of the update
monoid of every reachable exact predictive agent.
\end{corollary}

This is the result for agents.  Different exact predictors may make
implementation distinctions that $\Phi$ identifies, but none can
avoid $S_p$ as a quotient.  We write $\kappa=\ker\Phi$ for the kernel
of $\Phi$, the relation $\Phi(s)=\Phi(t)$ on $S_A$.  It is a
congruence: an equivalence relation compatible with multiplication on
both sides.

\begin{remark}[Uniformly accurate predictors]
\label{rem:uniform}
When $X_p$ is finite, exactness can be weakened.  Fix any metric on
future laws and let $\gamma>0$ be the least distance between two
distinct elements of $X_p$.  Suppose an agent satisfies every
condition of the definition except exactness, and its readout obeys
$d(F(\delta_u(q_0)),q_u)<\gamma/2$ for every possible $u$.  Then
$q_u$ is the unique element of $X_p$ within $\gamma/2$ of the
readout, so composing $F$ with rounding to the nearest element of
$X_p$ gives an exact agent with the same states and updates.  Every
result below that is stated for exact agents therefore holds for
agents accurate to within $\gamma/2$ on all possible histories.  The
accuracy must be uniform: a predictor that is accurate on most
histories only is not covered (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{remark}

\section{The possibility layer}
\label{sec:support}

The predictive state $q_u$ records how likely each future is.  A coarser
record keeps only which futures are possible at all, we call this
bare possibility.  This section shows
that this coarser structure is itself a canonical machine, sitting below
the predictive one, so that every exact predictor has a second, coarser
forced quotient.

\begin{definition}[Follower sets]
For $p(u)>0$, the follower set of $u$ is the set of futures it leaves
possible,
\[
  F_u=\{v\in\Obs^*:p(uv)>0\}.
\]
The support states of $p$ are
\[
  X_{\mathrm{supp}}=\{F_u:p(u)>0\},
  \qquad
  \widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}}
  =X_{\mathrm{supp}}\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{\dagger\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Each $F_u$ contains $\eps$ and is closed under prefixes.  The update on
follower sets is the same one-symbol shift as before:
\[
  \sigma_o(F)=
  \begin{cases}
    \{v:ov\in F\},&o\in F,\\
    \dagger,&o\notin F,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \sigma_o(\dagger)=\dagger.
\]
Unlike the predictive update, no well-definedness check is needed: the
right-hand side is a function of the set $F$ itself, and
$\sigma_o(F_u)=F_{uo}$ whenever $p(uo)>0$, while $o\notin F_u$ exactly
when $p(uo)=0$.  Let
\[
  S_{\mathrm{supp}}
  =\langle\sigma_o:o\in\Obs\rangle
  \subseteq\mathcal T_{\widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}}},
\]
with the same composition convention as $S_p$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:support-quotient}
The map
\[
  \sigma:\widehat X_p\to\widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}},
  \qquad
  \sigma(q_u)=F_u,
  \qquad
  \sigma(\dagger)=\dagger,
\]
is a well-defined surjection satisfying
$\sigma\circ\tau_o=\sigma_o\circ\sigma$ for every $o\in\Obs$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$F_u=\{v:q_u(v)>0\}$ is determined by the function $q_u$ alone, so
$\sigma$ is well-defined, and it is surjective because every follower set
arises from some possible $u$.  For the displayed equation at a live
state: if $p(uo)>0$ both sides equal $F_{uo}$, and if $p(uo)=0$ then
$q_u(o)=0$, so $o\notin F_u$ and both sides equal $\dagger$.  At
$\dagger$ both sides are $\dagger$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:support-tower}
Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} applied to $\sigma$ gives a surjective monoid
homomorphism $S_p\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$, whose kernel we write
$\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}$.  Composing with
Theorem~\ref{thm:predictor-quotient}, every reachable exact predictive
agent $A$ has a chain of quotients
\[
  S_A\longrightarrow S_p\longrightarrow S_{\mathrm{supp}},
\]
and the kernel $\kappa'$ of the composite contains
$\kappa=\ker\Phi$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The composite of the two equivariant surjections
$Q\to\widehat X_p\to\widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}}$ (equivariant meaning
commuting with every update map) is again an equivariant surjection, so Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} applies to each arrow and to the
composite, the kernels nest because the composite identifies at least as
much as the first factor.
\end{proof}

Every exact predictor therefore has two layers of forced structure:
a coarse quotient recording distinctions of possibility, and above it
a finer one recording distinctions of conditional expectation.  The
coarse layer is purely combinatorial: it depends only on the set
$L_p$, not on any probability value.\footnote{In the terminology of symbolic
dynamics, $\widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}}$ with the updates $\sigma_o$ is
the follower-set automaton of the language $L_p$, for stationary
processes whose support is a sofic shift this is a standard object
\cite{LindMarcus1995}, and the quotient $\sigma$ is the finite-history
analogue of the relation between the history $\varepsilon$-machine and
the Krieger cover \cite{TraversCrutchfield2025}.  In this section
the layer enters only as a second instance of Lemma~\ref{lem:descent},
Lemma~\ref{lem:syntactic} later identifies $S_{\mathrm{supp}}$ with
the syntactic monoid of $L_p$.}



\begin{remark}[When the layer is trivial]
\label{rem:full-support}
$S_{\mathrm{supp}}$ is the trivial monoid exactly when every finite word
is possible.  If $p(w)>0$ for all $w$, then $F_u=\Obs^*$ for every $u$,
there is a single support state, and every $\sigma_o$ is the identity.
Conversely, if some word is impossible, take a shortest impossible $wo$
with $p(w)>0$, then $\sigma_o$ sends $F_w$ to $\dagger$ and is not the
identity.  In particular, the full-support Markov chains of
Section~\ref{sec:reset} have a trivial possibility layer: their entire
canonical structure lives in the predictive layer.  A process can thus force rich structure at one layer
and none at the other.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Two computed examples: the bottom layer can be everything}
\label{subsec:computed-support}

The quotient $S_p\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$ of
Corollary~\ref{cor:support-tower} can degenerate in either direction.
Remark~\ref{rem:full-support} gave one extreme, where it collapses
everything, the opposite occurs in two small hidden-Markov processes
used as standard examples in the interpretability literature
\cite{ShaiEtAl2024}.  The first, Z1R, cycles deterministically through
``0, then 1, then a fair coin''.  The second, RRXOR, emits two fair
coins and then their exclusive-or, repeatedly.  Both have impossible
words, so neither has a trivial possibility layer.

For each process we computed, in exact rational arithmetic from the
stationary initial condition, the predictive states, the canonical
monoid, and the support quotient, scripts and outputs are in the
repository \cite{DovetailRepo}.  For Z1R: $|X_p|=7$ and $|S_p|=14$.
For RRXOR: $|X_p|=36$ and $|S_p|=73$.  In both processes the
state map $\sigma$ is genuinely lossy: Z1R merges one pair of
predictive states, and RRXOR merges its $36$ live states into $20$
follower-set classes.  Yet in both, the monoid map
$S_p\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$ is an \emph{isomorphism}:
$\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}$ is the equality relation.  Words that act
identically on bare possibility already act identically on prediction.

Two consequences.  First, states and transformations separate cleanly:
coarse-graining the states loses information, while the induced map
of transformations loses none.  Second, for these processes every
invariant of $S_p$ is an invariant of the support language $L_p$
alone, and is read off without consulting a single probability value.

Together with Remark~\ref{rem:full-support}, this exhibits both poles.
The full-support Markov chains of Section~\ref{sec:reset} have
$\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}=\top$, and Z1R and RRXOR have
$\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}=\bot$.  The next example lies strictly
between.

\begin{example}[A strictly intermediate kernel]
\label{ex:one-zero}
Let $p$ be the first-order Markov chain on $\Obs=\{1,2,3\}$ with
uniform initial distribution and transition matrix
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0&\frac12&\frac12\\[2pt]
    \frac12&\frac16&\frac13\\[2pt]
    \frac13&\frac12&\frac16
  \end{pmatrix},
\]
whose only zero forbids the word $11$.  As in
Section~\ref{sec:reset}, the live predictive states are $q_\eps$ and
the three states $g_1,g_2,g_3$ after a last symbol, pairwise distinct
because the rows are distinct and none is uniform.  The updates
$\tau_2$ and $\tau_3$ reset every live state to $g_2$ and to $g_3$,
while $\tau_1$ sends $g_1$ to $\dagger$ and every other live state to
$g_1$.  There are two follower sets: after a history ending in $1$,
the words that avoid $11$ and do not begin with $1$, and after every
other history, including the empty one, all words that avoid $11$.
So $\sigma$ merges $q_\eps,g_2,g_3$.

Then $\tau_2\ne\tau_3$ but $\sigma_2=\sigma_3$, so
$\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}\ne\bot$, and $\sigma_1$ is not the identity,
so $\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}\ne\top$.  Exact enumeration gives
$|S_p|=8$ and $|S_{\mathrm{supp}}|=6$, the kernel merging $\tau_2$
with $\tau_3$ and $\tau_{12}$ with $\tau_{13}$ and nothing else.  Any
transition matrix with distinct rows and a single zero on the
diagonal behaves the same way.
\end{example}

\section{The syntactic reading and what transports}
\label{sec:syntactic}

Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient} makes $S_p$ a quotient of every exact
predictor's update monoid.  This section identifies $S_p$ and
$S_{\mathrm{supp}}$ with classical objects of language theory, and
then records the invariants that pass along the surjection
$\Phi:S_A\to S_p$ when the predictor is finite: subgroups,
aperiodicity, and group complexity.

\subsection{Both canonical monoids are syntactic}

Let $f:\Obs^*\to Y$ be any map.  Its syntactic congruence is
\[
  w\equiv_f w'
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  f(uwv)=f(uw'v)\ \text{ for all }u,v\in\Obs^*,
\]
and its syntactic monoid is $M(f)=\Obs^*/{\equiv_f}$.  For a language
$K\subseteq\Obs^*$ write $\equiv_K$ and $M(K)$ for the congruence and
monoid of its indicator function, this is the usual syntactic monoid
of $K$ \cite{Pin1986}.

Define the state labelling of $p$ by
\[
  \nu:\Obs^*\to\widehat X_p,
  \qquad
  \nu(u)=
  \begin{cases}
    q_u,&p(u)>0,\\
    \dagger,&p(u)=0.
  \end{cases}
\]
Then $\nu(u)=\tau_u(q_\eps)$ for every word $u$: if $p(u)>0$ this is
the definition of $\tau_u$, and otherwise the shortest impossible
prefix of $u$ sends $q_\eps$ to $\dagger$, which is absorbing.  For a
live state $x\in X_p$ write
\[
  K_x=\{u\in\Obs^*:\nu(u)=x\}
\]
for the set of histories that lead to $x$.

\begin{lemma}[Syntactic lemma]
\label{lem:syntactic}
For all words $w,w'$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $\tau_w=\tau_{w'}$ if and only if $w\equiv_\nu w'$.  Hence
        $\tau_w\mapsto[w]$ is a bijection $S_p\to M(\nu)$, and it
        reverses products, so $S_p\cong M(\nu)^{\mathrm{op}}$.
  \item $\sigma_w=\sigma_{w'}$ if and only if $w\equiv_{L_p}w'$.
        Hence $S_{\mathrm{supp}}\cong M(L_p)^{\mathrm{op}}$.
  \item $\equiv_\nu\;=\bigcap_{x\in X_p}\equiv_{K_x}$.  Hence each
        $M(K_x)$ is a quotient of $M(\nu)$, and $M(\nu)$ embeds in the
        product $\prod_{x\in X_p}M(K_x)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Both $\tau_w$ and $\tau_{w'}$ fix $\dagger$, and every live state
is $\nu(u)$ for some $u$ with $p(u)>0$, with
$\tau_w(\nu(u))=\nu(uw)$.  If $p(u)=0$ then
$\nu(uw)=\nu(uw')=\dagger$.  So $\tau_w=\tau_{w'}$ exactly when
$\nu(uw)=\nu(uw')$ for every $u\in\Obs^*$.  This is the syntactic
condition with $v=\eps$, and it implies the condition for every $v$
because $\nu(uwv)=\tau_v(\nu(uw))$.  The map reverses products because
$\tau_w\circ\tau_v=\tau_{vw}$.

(ii) Extend the follower-set notation to all words by
$F_u=\{v:uv\in L_p\}$, which is empty exactly when $u\notin L_p$,
since $\eps\in F_u$ otherwise.  Reading $\dagger$ as the empty set,
the argument of (i) applied to $u\mapsto F_u$ and the updates
$\sigma_o$ shows that $\sigma_w=\sigma_{w'}$ exactly when
$F_{uw}=F_{uw'}$ for every $u$.  That is the statement that
$uwv\in L_p$ if and only if $uw'v\in L_p$, for all $u,v$.

(iii) If $w\equiv_\nu w'$ then $\nu(uwv)=x$ if and only if
$\nu(uw'v)=x$, so $w\equiv_{K_x}w'$.  Conversely suppose
$w\equiv_{K_x}w'$ for every live $x$, and fix $u,v$.  If
$\nu(uwv)=x$ is live, then $uwv\in K_x$, so $uw'v\in K_x$ and
$\nu(uw'v)=x$, and symmetrically.  If neither value is live, both are
$\dagger$.
\end{proof}

No finiteness is used.  When $X_p$ is finite, part (i) says that
$\widehat X_p$ is the minimal automaton of the labelling $\nu$ and
$S_p$ is its transition monoid, and part (ii) says the same of
$\widehat X_{\mathrm{supp}}$ and the language $L_p$ of possible
words.  The question of
Section~\ref{subsec:computed-support}, when $S_p\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$
is an isomorphism, therefore asks when the labelling $\nu$ and its
support $L_p$ have the same syntactic congruence.  Passing to the
opposite monoid changes none of the invariants used in this paper:
congruences, subgroups up to isomorphism, and aperiodicity are the
same for $S$ and $S^{\mathrm{op}}$.

\begin{remark}[States and word problem]
\label{rem:word-problem}
Computing the predictive state of a history and multiplying in $S_p$
are two forms of the same task.  The state is
$\nu(u)=\tau_u(q_\eps)$, one product in $S_p$ applied to one point.
Conversely, when $X_p$ is finite, fix a history $u_x$ with
$\nu(u_x)=x$ for each live $x$, then $\tau_w$ is the tuple
$(\nu(u_xw))_{x\in X_p}$, that is, $|X_p|$ state computations with
fixed prefixes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Transport to finite predictors}

A subgroup of a monoid $S$ is a subsemigroup that is a group, its
identity is an idempotent of $S$ and need not be the identity of $S$.
A finite monoid is aperiodic when all its subgroups are trivial,
equivalently when for every $s$ there is $n$ with $s^n=s^{n+1}$.  A
monoid $T$ divides $S$ when $T$ is a quotient of a submonoid of $S$.
The group complexity $c(S)$ of a finite monoid is the least number of
group factors in a wreath product of groups and aperiodic monoids,
taken alternately, that $S$ divides, it is finite by the
Krohn--Rhodes theorem, and it is monotone under division
\cite{KrohnRhodes1965,RhodesSteinberg2009}.

\begin{theorem}[Transport]
\label{thm:transport}
Let $A$ be a reachable exact predictive agent for $p$ whose update
monoid $S_A$ is finite, as it is whenever $Q$ is finite.  Then $S_p$
is finite, and:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item for every subgroup $G\subseteq S_p$ there is a subgroup
        $H\subseteq S_A$ with $\Phi(H)=G$, in particular every group
        that divides $S_p$ divides $S_A$,
  \item if $S_A$ is aperiodic then $S_p$ is aperiodic,
  \item $c(S_p)\le c(S_A)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$S_p=\Phi(S_A)$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:quotient}, so it is
finite and divides $S_A$.

(i) The preimage $\Phi^{-1}(G)$ is a finite subsemigroup of $S_A$
mapping onto $G$.  Choose a subsemigroup $H\subseteq\Phi^{-1}(G)$
minimal subject to $\Phi(H)=G$.  For $a\in H$, the set $aH$ is a
subsemigroup of $H$ with $\Phi(aH)=\Phi(a)G=G$, so $aH=H$ by
minimality, and likewise $Ha=H$.  A semigroup in which $aH=H=Ha$ for
every $a$ is a group.  The second statement holds because division is
transitive.

(ii) If $s^n=s^{n+1}$ in $S_A$ then
$\Phi(s)^n=\Phi(s)^{n+1}$, and $\Phi$ is onto.

(iii) Monotonicity of $c$ under division.
\end{proof}

Finiteness of $S_A$ is used only to lift a subgroup in (i) and to
define the complexity in (iii).  The second statement of (i) and, with
aperiodicity read as the condition that every $s$ has an $n$ with
$s^n=s^{n+1}$, part (ii) hold for every reachable exact predictor,
because they use only that $S_p$ is a quotient of $S_A$.
Example~\ref{ex:free-predictor} shows that the subgroup lifting in (i)
does fail without finiteness.

Part (i) gives a subgroup mapping onto $G$, not a copy of $G$: a
predictor may cover a two-element group in $S_p$ by a four-element
cyclic group.  What cannot be avoided is $G$ as a divisor.  For a
simple non-abelian group such as $A_5$ this means that $A_5$ is a
composition factor of some subgroup of $S_A$.

\begin{remark}[The bounds are attained]
\label{rem:tight}
The canonical machine
$(\widehat X_p,q_\eps,\dagger,\{\tau_o\},\id)$ is itself a reachable
exact predictive agent, with update monoid $S_p$.  So when $X_p$ is
finite, the minimum of $c(S_A)$ over finite exact predictors is
exactly $c(S_p)$, and $S_p$ is aperiodic if and only if some finite
exact predictor has an aperiodic update monoid.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Finiteness cannot be dropped]
\label{ex:free-predictor}
Let $\Obs=\{0,1\}$ and let $p$ emit independent symbols, a fair coin
at odd positions and a coin with bias $\tfrac13$ at even positions.
Every word is possible, there are two predictive states according to
the parity of the history length, and both symbols exchange them, so
$S_p$ is the two-element group.  Now let $A$ store the entire
history: $Q^+=\Obs^*$, $\delta_o(u)=uo$, and $F(u)=q_u$.  This is a
reachable exact predictive agent, and $\delta_w=\delta_{w'}$ only
when $w=w'$, so its update monoid is the free monoid on two
generators.
That monoid has no nontrivial subgroup, so the conclusion of
Theorem~\ref{thm:transport}(i) fails for it, and since every finite
monoid generated by two elements is a quotient of it, division by
$S_A$ constrains nothing.  A predictor with unbounded memory can
avoid every group in $S_p$ by never merging histories.  The example
also shows that a group in $S_p$ need not be hard to compute: this
one only counts the length of the history modulo two.
\end{example}

The reset chains of Section~\ref{sec:reset} are a first check on
these invariants.  The monoid $R_n^1$ is aperiodic, every element
satisfies $s^2=s$, so the last-symbol predictor that carries an $M_3$
congruence interval is trivial for all three parts of
Theorem~\ref{thm:transport}.

\subsection{Predictors without a recurrent state}
\label{subsec:parallel}

Theorem~\ref{thm:transport} concerns agents that update a state, and
Example~\ref{ex:free-predictor} shows it says nothing once memory is
unbounded.  A model that reads the whole history at once, such as a
transformer, is in that position: it has no update monoid to divide.
For such models the constraint has to be placed on the function
computed, and Lemma~\ref{lem:syntactic} with
Remark~\ref{rem:word-problem} supplies it through circuit complexity.

Assume throughout this subsection that $X_p$ is finite.  The
next-symbol law of a possible history is
\[
  n_p(u)=\bigl(q_u(o)\bigr)_{o\in\Obs}
  \qquad(u\in L_p),
\]
which depends on $u$ only through $\nu(u)$ and so takes finitely many
values.  A family of functions, one for each input length,
\emph{predicts $p$} when it returns $n_p(u)$ on every possible word
$u$, nothing is required on impossible words.  The \emph{word
problem} of $S_p$ is the task of computing $\tau_w$ from $w$.  We use
the standard classes
$\mathrm{AC}^0\subseteq\mathrm{ACC}^0\subseteq\mathrm{TC}^0
\subseteq\mathrm{NC}^1$ of constant-depth circuit families with
unbounded fan-in gates (respectively: and/or, with modular counting
gates added, with threshold gates added) and of logarithmic-depth
bounded fan-in families.  A reduction is a constant-depth,
polynomial-size circuit with oracle gates.

\begin{proposition}[Prediction and the word problem]
\label{prop:pred-word}
Assume $X_p$ is finite.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item $n_p(u)$ is a function of $\tau_u$, namely the next-symbol
        law of the state $\tau_u(q_\eps)$.
  \item The word problem of $S_p$ reduces to predicting $p$: there
        is a constant $H$ such that $\tau_w$ is computed by a
        constant-depth circuit of size $O(|w|)$ from the values of
        any family that predicts $p$, queried at the words
        $u_xw'v$, where $x$ ranges over $X_p$, $u_x$ is a fixed
        history with $\nu(u_x)=x$, $w'$ over the prefixes of $w$,
        and $v$ over the words of length less than $H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) is $\nu(u)=\tau_u(q_\eps)$.

(ii) By Remark~\ref{rem:word-problem} it suffices to compute
$\nu(u_xw)$ for each $x$.  First decide whether $u_xw$ is possible.
Write $w=o_1\cdots o_m$ and let $c_i$ be the $o_i$-coordinate of the
value returned at $u_xo_1\cdots o_{i-1}$.  Then $u_xw\in L_p$ exactly
when every $c_i$ is positive.  If $u_xw$ is possible, all the queried
words are possible and the values are correct.  If it is not, let $i$
be least with $u_xo_1\cdots o_i\notin L_p$, the query at
$u_xo_1\cdots o_{i-1}$ is at a possible word and returns $c_i=0$, so
the conjunction fails whatever the later queries return.

If $u_xw$ is possible, its state is found as follows.  Since $X_p$ is
finite and distinct states differ at some finite future, there is $H$
such that the values $q(v)$ with $|v|\le H$ determine $q\in X_p$.
For $u\in L_p$ and $v=v_1\cdots v_k$,
\[
  q_u(v)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{uv_1\cdots v_{i-1}}(v_i),
\]
read as zero from the first vanishing factor on, and each factor is
a coordinate of $n_p$ at a possible word.  So the values returned at
the words $u_xwv$ with $|v|<H$ determine $\nu(u_xw)$ through a fixed
finite table.  The conjunction has depth one and size $O(|w|)$, and
the table has constant size.
\end{proof}

Since $n_p$ takes finitely many values, the reduction only needs each
returned value rounded to the nearest of them.  The proposition
therefore applies unchanged to any family whose output is at
distance less than
$\gamma/2$ from $n_p(u)$ on every possible $u$, where $\gamma$ is the
least distance between two distinct values of $n_p$.

\begin{theorem}[Complexity of exact prediction]
\label{thm:pred-complexity}
Assume $X_p$ is finite, and write
$\tau(\Obs^t)=\{\tau_w:|w|=t\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Predicting $p$ is in $\mathrm{NC}^1$.  It is in
        $\mathrm{AC}^0$ if $S_p$ is aperiodic, and in
        $\mathrm{ACC}^0$ if every subgroup of $S_p$ is solvable.
  \item If for some $t$ the set $\tau(\Obs^t)$ contains a nontrivial
        group, then predicting $p$ is not in $\mathrm{AC}^0$.
  \item If $S_p$ contains a non-solvable group, then predicting $p$
        is $\mathrm{NC}^1$-complete.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) By Proposition~\ref{prop:pred-word}(i), predicting $p$ reduces to
the word problem of the finite monoid $S_p$, which is in
$\mathrm{NC}^1$, in $\mathrm{AC}^0$ for aperiodic monoids, and in
$\mathrm{ACC}^0$ for monoids all of whose subgroups are solvable
\cite{BarringtonTherien1988,BarringtonEtAl1992}.

(ii) Let $G\subseteq\tau(\Obs^t)$ be a nontrivial group with identity
$e$, and let $g\in G$ have prime order $r$.  Choose words $w_e,w_g$
of length $t$ with $\tau_{w_e}=e$ and $\tau_{w_g}=g$.  Given bits
$b_1,\ldots,b_n$, substitute $w_g$ for each $1$ and $w_e$ for each
$0$.  The resulting word $w$ of length $tn$ has
$\tau_w=g^{\,b_1+\cdots+b_n}$, which equals $e$ exactly when the sum
is divisible by $r$.  So counting modulo $r$ reduces to the word
problem of $S_p$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:pred-word}(ii) to
predicting $p$.  Counting modulo $r$ is not in $\mathrm{AC}^0$
\cite{Smolensky1987}.

(iii) Membership is (i).  For hardness, a non-solvable finite group
has a nontrivial perfect subgroup, the last term of its derived
series, so let $G\subseteq S_p$ be perfect and nontrivial with
identity $e$.  We show $G\subseteq\tau(\Obs^t)$ for some $t$.  Fix a
word $w_e$ for $e$, of length $a$, and let
$D\subseteq\mathbb Z/a$ be the set of residues of lengths of words
$w$ with $\tau_w=e$.  It is nonempty and closed under addition, so it
is a subgroup.  If $w,w'$ both represent $g\in G$ and $v$ represents
$g^{-1}$, then $wv$ and $w'v$ represent $e$, so $|w|-|w'|\in D$.
Hence $g\mapsto|w|+D$ is a well-defined map
$G\to(\mathbb Z/a)/D$, and it respects products because lengths add
under concatenation.  Its target is abelian and $G$ is perfect, so
it is trivial: every $g\in G$ has a word whose length lies in $D$
modulo $a$.  Appending a word for $e$ of the opposite residue, and
then copies of $w_e$, gives words of one common length $t$ for all
elements of $G$.  Substituting these words for group elements
reduces the word problem of $G$ to that of $S_p$, and so to
predicting $p$.  The word problem of a non-solvable group is
$\mathrm{NC}^1$-complete under $\mathrm{AC}^0$ reductions
\cite{Barrington1989}.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis of (ii) is about words of equal length, and it cannot
be weakened to ``$S_p$ is not aperiodic''.  In
Example~\ref{ex:free-predictor}, $S_p$ is the two-element group, but
$\tau(\Obs^t)$ is a single element for every $t$, and the process is
predicted by a function of the input length alone.  The proof of
(iii) shows that a non-solvable group never escapes this way.

\begin{corollary}[Constant-depth transformers]
\label{cor:transformers}
Assume $X_p$ is finite and $S_p$ contains a non-solvable group.  If
$\mathrm{TC}^0\ne\mathrm{NC}^1$, then no family of
constant-depth transformers with logarithmic precision predicts $p$
on all possible words of all lengths, nor comes within $\gamma/2$ of
doing so.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Such transformers compute only functions in uniform $\mathrm{TC}^0$
\cite{MerrillSabharwal2023}, hence in $\mathrm{TC}^0$, and $\mathrm{TC}^0$ is closed under the
reductions used above, so a predicting family would place an
$\mathrm{NC}^1$-complete problem in $\mathrm{TC}^0$.
\end{proof}

In the other direction, Lemma~\ref{lem:syntactic}(iii) shows that
$S_p$ is aperiodic exactly when every $M(K_x)$ is, since quotients
and subdirect products of aperiodic monoids are aperiodic.  By
Sch\"utzenberger's theorem \cite{Schutzenberger1965} that is the
statement that every language $K_x$ of histories leading to a given
state is star-free, and star-free languages are exactly those
recognised in the masked hard-attention transformer model of
\cite{YangChiangAngluin2024}.

Three limits apply to Corollary~\ref{cor:transformers}.  It is
asymptotic in the length: at any fixed length $T$, every finite-state
update can be simulated by a transformer of depth $O(\log T)$, and
of constant depth when all subgroups are solvable
\cite{LiuEtAl2023}.  It is conditional on
$\mathrm{TC}^0\ne\mathrm{NC}^1$.  And it is a worst-case statement
over possible histories: a predictor that is accurate on most
histories under $p$, but wrong on a set of small probability, is not
covered, and we do not know what an average-case version should say.

\section{How much must be remembered}
\label{sec:rank}

The invariants of Section~\ref{sec:syntactic} are global.  This
section extracts a word by word one: for each word $w$, a lower bound on the number
of distinctions any exact predictor must still be holding after reading
$w$.

\begin{definition}[Live rank]
For $s\in S_p$, the live rank of $s$ is the number of live states in its
image,
\[
  \rk(s)=\bigl|\,s(\widehat X_p)\cap X_p\,\bigr|
  \in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Composition cannot increase live rank on either side.  For $s\circ t$,
the image of the composite is contained in the image of $s$, so
$\rk(s\circ t)\le\rk(s)$, and the image of $s\circ t$ is
$s(\mathrm{im}\,t)$, whose live part is contained in the image under $s$
of the live part of $\mathrm{im}\,t$ (since $s(\dagger)=\dagger$ is not
live), so $\rk(s\circ t)\le\rk(t)$ as well.

For a word $w$, the live states in the image of $\tau_w$ are exactly the
predictive states $q_{uw}$ over histories $u$ with $p(uw)>0$.  So
$\rk(\tau_w)$ counts the distinct conditional future laws that can hold
immediately after an occurrence of $w$.

\begin{theorem}[Inherited memory bound]
\label{thm:rank-bound}
Let $A$ be a reachable exact predictive agent for $p$.  For every word
$w\in\Obs^*$,
\[
  \rk(\tau_w)\ \le\
  \bigl|\,\delta_w(Q)\cap Q^+\,\bigr|.
\]
The right-hand side is the number of distinct live agent states
reachable by a history ending in $w$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:predictor-quotient},
$L\circ\delta_w=\tau_w\circ L$ with $L$ surjective, so
$\tau_w(\widehat X_p)=L(\delta_w(Q))$.  A live element of the left-hand
side is the image under $L$ of some element of $\delta_w(Q)$, and that
element must lie in $Q^+$ because $L(d)=\dagger$.  So $L$ maps
$\delta_w(Q)\cap Q^+$ onto $\tau_w(\widehat X_p)\cap X_p$, giving the
inequality.  For the interpretation: $Q=\{\delta_u(q_0):p(u)>0\}
\mathbin{\dot\cup}\{d\}$ by reachability, so
$\delta_w(Q)\cap Q^+=\{\delta_{uw}(q_0):p(uw)>0\}\cap Q^+$, the live
states reachable by histories with final block $w$.
\end{proof}

The bound is determined by the process alone and holds word by word for
every exact predictor, whatever its implementation.  Its two extremes
have names and appear as the two poles of this paper's examples.

\begin{example}[Total forgetting: the reset chain]
Call $w$ synchronising when $\rk(\tau_w)=1$: after reading $w$, a single
conditional future law holds, regardless of what came before, so an
exact predictor is permitted to forget everything preceding $w$.  In the
Markov chains of Proposition~\ref{prop:markov-reset-general} every
single symbol is synchronising: $\rk(c_i)=1$ for each reset map, and
only the identity has full live rank.  This is the precise content of
calling the last-symbol predictor transparent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[No forgetting: the P\'olya urn]
\label{ex:polya}
Let $\Obs=\{\mathsf r,\mathsf b\}$ and let $p$ be the P\'olya urn started
from one red and one blue ball: each symbol is drawn with probability
proportional to the current count of its colour, and the drawn colour's
count then increases by one.  Explicitly, any word with $r$ reds and $b$
blues has
\[
  p(w)=\frac{r!\,b!}{(r+b+1)!}>0,
\]
so the process has full support and the order of symbols does not
matter.  The predictive state after a history is therefore a function of
the pair $(r,b)$ alone.  Distinct pairs give distinct states, by two
finite-future probabilities: with $n=r+b+2$,
\[
  q_{(r,b)}(\mathsf r)=\frac{r+1}{n},
  \qquad
  q_{(r,b)}(\mathsf r\mathsf r)=\frac{(r+1)(r+2)}{n(n+1)},
\]
and these two values determine first $n$ and then $r$.  One-step
probabilities alone do not suffice: $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$ both predict
red with probability $\tfrac12$, but their two-step predictions
differ, $\tfrac{3}{10}$ against $\tfrac27$.

The updates act on states by
$(r,b)\mapsto(r+1,b)$ and $(r,b)\mapsto(r,b+1)$, so every $\tau_w$ is
injective (Figure~\ref{fig:urn}) and
\[
  \rk(\tau_w)=\infty
  \qquad(w\in\Obs^*).
\]

No word is synchronising, and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-bound} no exact predictor of this process can
bound its number of states: it must hold unboundedly many distinctions

Section~\ref{sec:linear} is infinite as well, nothing in this section
assumed finiteness.
\end{example}

The reset chain and the urn are opposite answers to the same question.
In one, the world licenses total forgetting at every step and the forced
memory is a single symbol, in the other, the world licenses no merging
at all and the forced memory grows without bound.  The quantity
$\rk(\tau_w)$ interpolates between them word by word.\footnote{The sets $I_k=\{s\in S_p:\rk(s)\le k\}$ are two-sided ideals, so the rank profile also stratifies
$S_p$ into Rees quotients, each again contributing an inherited
interval via congruence correspondence, we do not pursue this here.}

The rank profile also ties the memory bound to the subgroups of
Section~\ref{sec:syntactic}.

\begin{proposition}[Groups sit at a rank level]
\label{prop:group-rank}
Let $G\subseteq S_p$ be a subgroup with identity $e$, and let
$I=e(\widehat X_p)\cap X_p$ be the live image of $e$.  Every $g\in G$
has live image $I$, and restriction to $I$ is an injective
homomorphism $G\to\mathrm{Sym}(I)$.  In particular $\rk(g)=\rk(e)$
for all $g\in G$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $g\in G$ with inverse $g^{-1}$ in $G$ we have $g=e\circ g$ and
$e=g\circ g^{-1}$, so the images of $g$ and $e$ contain each other.
The idempotent $e$ fixes its image pointwise.  So $g$ maps the image
of $e$ into itself, and $g\circ g^{-1}=g^{-1}\circ g=e$ is the
identity there, hence $g$ permutes the image of $e$.  It fixes
$\dagger$, so it permutes $I$.  Restriction respects composition, and
it is injective because $g=g\circ e$ is determined by its values on
the image of $e$.
\end{proof}

Combined with Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-bound}: if $\tau_w$ lies in a
subgroup $G$ of $S_p$, every exact predictor holds at least as many
live states after $w$ as the smallest set on which $G$ acts
faithfully.  A copy of $A_5$ in $S_p$ forces five, on every word
whose update lies in it, and this half of the group constraint needs
no finiteness of the predictor, unlike
Theorem~\ref{thm:transport}.

Synchronisation is a studied notion on both sides of this construction.
For $\varepsilon$-machines, Travers and Crutchfield analyse when and
how fast an observer of a stationary finite-state source pins down the
predictive state, including a polynomial-time test for the existence of
a synchronising word
\cite{TraversCrutchfield2011a,TraversCrutchfield2011b}, for
deterministic automata, reset words are the subject of a large
literature around the \v{C}ern\'y conjecture \cite{Volkov2008}.  The
contribution of this section is neither notion but the transport:
Theorem~\ref{thm:rank-bound} carries the full rank profile, not only
the synchronising case, to every exact predictor of the process, with
no stationarity or finiteness hypothesis.


\section{The finite-rank linear realisation}
\label{sec:linear}

The preceding sections used no linear structure.  This section builds
the canonical \emph{linear} predictor of $p$: a vector space carrying
one operator per symbol, whose word products reproduce every
probability.  Its operator monoid is the object whose comparison with
$S_p$, in Section~\ref{sec:quantitative}, isolates the likelihood
layer.  The construction is classical when $p$ has finite Hankel rank
\cite{Fliess1974,Jaeger2000}.

Let
\[
  W=\R^{\Obs^*}
\]
be the vector space of all real functions on finite futures, we never
choose a basis for $W$.  Define the unnormalised row
\[
  r_u(v)=p(uv),
\]
the $u$-th row of the infinite matrix $H_{u,v}=p(uv)$, classically
called the Hankel matrix of $p$ because its entries depend only on the
concatenation $uv$.  Let
\[
  V=\spn\{r_u:u\in\Obs^*\}\subseteq W,
\]
and define
\[
  \rank H:=\dim\spn\{r_u:u\in\Obs^*\}.
\]
Assume in this section that $\rank H<\infty$.  Then $\dim V=\rank H$.

For $o\in\Obs$, define
\[
  (T_of)(v)=f(ov).
\]
Then $T_or_u=r_{uo}$, so $T_o$ restricts to a linear endomorphism of
$V$.  Use a column convention.  For $w=o_1\cdots o_n$, put
\[
  T_w=T_{o_n}\cdots T_{o_1},
\]
then $T_wr_u=r_{uw}$.  Hence $w\mapsto T_w$ is a homomorphism into the
opposite monoid of $\End(V)$, this has no effect on congruence
lattices, as noted after Lemma~\ref{lem:descent}.  The readout is the
linear functional
\[
  \lambda:V\to\R,\qquad\lambda(f)=f(\eps),
\]
and
\[
  p(w)=\lambda(T_wr_\eps).
\]

The rows $r_u$ are not conditional predictive states: for $p(u)>0$,
\[
  r_u=p(u)q_u,
\]
a future law scaled by the probability of its history.  Two histories
with the same future law therefore give proportional rows, with a
positive ratio.  Call
\[
  [f]=\{cf:c>0\}
\]
the \emph{positive ray} through a nonzero $f\in V$, positivity matters,
since recovering $q_u$ from $r_u$ means normalising the
$\eps$-coordinate to one, which only a positive multiple permits.  The
assignment
\[
  \beta(q_u)=[r_u]
\]
is then a well-defined bijection from $X_p$ onto the set of rays of
nonzero rows, and $\beta$ intertwines the conditional and projective
updates:
\[
  \beta(\tau_o(q_u))=T_o\cdot\beta(q_u)
\]
whenever $p(uo)>0$, with impossible extensions sent to $\dagger$ on
both sides.  We henceforth identify $X_p$ with this ray set.

\begin{proposition}
The realisation $(V,r_\eps,\{T_o\},\lambda)$ is reachable and observable, and
therefore minimal.  Every reachable linear realisation of $p$ has dimension at
least $\dim V$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Reachability is the definition $V=\spn\{T_ur_\eps:u\in\Obs^*\}$.  If
$f\in V$ satisfies $\lambda(T_vf)=0$ for every $v$, then
$f(v)=0$ for every future $v$, so $f=0$, this is observability.  The
surjection constructed in Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-quotient} below gives
the dimension bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:linear-quotient}
Let $V'$ be a vector space, $x_0\in V'$, $A_o\in\End(V')$, and
$\lambda'\in(V')^*$.  For $w=o_1\cdots o_n$, set
\[
  A_w=A_{o_n}\cdots A_{o_1}.
\]
Suppose these data are a reachable linear realisation of the same stochastic
language:
\[
  p(w)=\lambda'(A_wx_0)
  \quad(w\in\Obs^*),
  \qquad
  V'=\spn\{A_ux_0:u\in\Obs^*\}.
\]
Then there is a surjective linear map $L:V'\rightarrow V$ satisfying
\[
  LA_o=T_oL
  \qquad(o\in\Obs).
\]
It therefore induces a surjective homomorphism from the agent's operator monoid
onto the minimal operator monoid.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $x\in V'$, define its future behaviour by
\[
  (Lx)(v)=\lambda'(A_vx).
\]
Reachability writes every $x$ as a linear combination of states $A_ux_0$, and
\[
  L(A_ux_0)=r_u.
\]
Thus $Lx\in V$ and $L$ is onto.  Moreover,
\[
  (LA_ox)(v)=\lambda'(A_vA_ox)
  =(T_oLx)(v),
\]
so $LA_o=T_oL$.  Since linear surjections are in particular surjections,
Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} applies to $L$ and gives the induced monoid map
\[
  \Psi:\langle A_o:o\in\Obs\rangle\rightarrow
  \langle T_o:o\in\Obs\rangle,
  \qquad
  \Psi(A_w)=T_w.
\]
\end{proof}

The result applies to exact weighted automata, observable-operator
models, and other reachable linear predictors of the complete joint
language.

\section{Conditional dynamics vs quantitative prediction}
\label{sec:quantitative}

Let $S_{\mathrm{lin}}=\langle T_o:o\in\Obs\rangle\subseteq\End(V)$.  Its action
on the positive Hankel rays is
\[
  T\cdot[r_u]=
  \begin{cases}
    [Tr_u],&Tr_u\ne0,\\
    \dagger,&Tr_u=0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad T\cdot\dagger=\dagger.
\]
This gives a monoid homomorphism
\[
  \alpha:S_{\mathrm{lin}}\longrightarrow
  \mathcal T_{\widehat X_p}.
\]
Its image is the canonical predictive transition monoid $S_p$ and
\[
  \kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}=\ker\alpha
\]
identifies operators that induce the same conditional state update, including
the same pattern of impossible continuations.

There is a simple computable lower bound.  Define
\[
  T\mathrel{\theta_{\mathrm{scal}}}T'
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  T'=cT\text{ for some }c>0.
\]
This is a congruence on $S_{\mathrm{lin}}$: positive scalar multiplication is
compatible with composition on both sides, and
\[
  \theta_{\mathrm{scal}}\subseteq\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}.
\]
The inclusion is strict in general: agreement on every ray fixes a
scalar for each ray separately, and nothing forces these to coincide.
Section~\ref{sec:reset} exhibits a process-realisable strict
inclusion.

The quotient by $\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}$ can forget quantitative
information.  For $x=[r_u]\in X_p$, define the likelihood cocycle
(cocycle meaning only that it composes by the chain rule displayed below)
\[
  \ell_T(x)=\frac{\lambda(Tr_u)}{\lambda(r_u)},
\]
with value $0$ when $Tr_u=0$, and set $\ell_T(\dagger)=1$.  This is independent
of the positive representative of the ray and satisfies
\[
  \ell_{TU}(x)=\ell_T(U\cdot x)\ell_U(x).
\]
If $T=T_w$, then
\[
  \ell_{T_w}([r_u])=\frac{p(uw)}{p(u)},
\]
the probability of observing $w$ from predictive state $q_u$, which
is the reason for the name.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:faithful}
If $T,T'\in S_{\mathrm{lin}}$ have the same action on $\widehat X_p$ and
\[
  \ell_T(x)=\ell_{T'}(x)
  \qquad(x\in X_p),
\]
then $T=T'$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $u$ with $p(u)>0$.  If $Tr_u=0$, equality of the ray actions gives
$T'r_u=0$.  Otherwise the two outputs lie on the same positive ray.  Equality of
likelihoods says that they also have the same value under $\lambda$.  A nonzero
Hankel row has positive $\eps$-coordinate, so its positive ray together with that
coordinate determines it uniquely.  Hence $Tr_u=T'r_u$.

The nonzero rows $r_u$ span $V$, zero rows contribute nothing.  Therefore $T$ and
$T'$ agree on a spanning set and are equal.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The likelihood layer}
\label{subsec:likelihood}

Proposition~\ref{prop:faithful} says an operator is determined by its
state update together with its likelihood function.  This subsection
packages that as a structure theorem: the operator monoid embeds in a
monoid built from exactly those two ingredients, the update skeleton
$S_p$ and the likelihoods, and the quotient
$S_p\cong S_{\mathrm{lin}}/\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is then literally a
coordinate projection.

Let
\[
  \mathcal N=(\R_{\ge0})^{\widehat X_p},
\]
the nonnegative functions on $\widehat X_p$, a commutative monoid under
pointwise multiplication with identity the constant function
$\mathbf 1$.  For $t\in S_p$, precomposition $f\mapsto f\circ t$
respects pointwise products and fixes $\mathbf 1$.

\begin{definition}
Let $\mathcal N\rtimes S_p$ be the set $\mathcal N\times S_p$ with
multiplication
\[
  (f,s)(g,t)=\bigl((f\circ t)\,g,\ st\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
$\mathcal N\rtimes S_p$ is a monoid with identity $(\mathbf 1,\id)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both bracketings of a triple product have second coordinate $stu$ and
first coordinate $(f\circ tu)(g\circ u)\,h$, using
$(f\circ t)\circ u=f\circ(tu)$ and that precomposition respects
pointwise products.  The identity checks are immediate.
\end{proof}

The order in the first coordinate is forced by the column convention:
the right factor's update acts first, so the left factor's weight must
be evaluated after it.

\begin{theorem}[Likelihood--skeleton embedding]
\label{thm:semidirect}
The map
\[
  \Lambda:S_{\mathrm{lin}}\longrightarrow\mathcal N\rtimes S_p,
  \qquad
  \Lambda(T)=(\ell_T,\alpha(T)),
\]
is an injective monoid homomorphism.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The cocycle identity $\ell_{TU}(x)=\ell_T(U\cdot x)\,\ell_U(x)$ holds
for every $x\in\widehat X_p$: at live states with $Ur_u\ne0$ it was
verified in the text, if $U$ kills $x$ both sides vanish, and at
$\dagger$ both sides equal $1$ (this is the reason for the convention
$\ell_T(\dagger)=1$).  It says exactly that first coordinates multiply
as in the definition, and second coordinates multiply because $\alpha$
is a homomorphism.  Injectivity is
Proposition~\ref{prop:faithful}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Invariants of $S_p$ read only the second coordinate]
\label{cor:projection}
Let $\pi:\mathcal N\rtimes S_p\to S_p$ be the projection.  Then
$\alpha=\pi\circ\Lambda$, so $\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is the pullback of
$\ker\pi$: two operators are $\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}$-related exactly
when their images under $\Lambda$ agree in the second coordinate.  Any
invariant computed from $S_p$, including the groups of
Section~\ref{sec:syntactic} and the congruence interval of
Section~\ref{sec:congruence}, is therefore blind to the
likelihood coordinate, the first component $\ell_T$ of $\Lambda(T)$,
by construction.
Section~\ref{subsec:computed-support} computes two processes where the
blindness is total: there $S_p$ carries no information beyond the
possibility layer.
\end{corollary}

The fibre of $\Lambda(S_{\mathrm{lin}})$ over $s\in S_p$, the set of
image elements with second coordinate $s$, consists of the likelihood
functions realised by operators with update $s$.
Section~\ref{sec:reset} computes a two element witness in one fibre:
$T_2$ and $T_{12}$ project to the same reset with distinct likelihood
functions.\footnote{For an i.i.d.\ process with all symbol
probabilities positive, $S_p$ is trivial and all content sits in a
single fibre: the opposite extreme.}

Sections~\ref{sec:agent}, \ref{sec:support}, and this one now assemble
into a tower, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:tower}.  A process forces structure at three
separable levels, what is possible, what is conditionally expected,
and how likely each transition is, and every invariant of $S_p$ reads
exactly the middle level (Corollary~\ref{cor:projection}), nothing here
decomposes $S_p$ itself in the cascade sense \cite{DrosteEtAl2011}.

\section{Why the congruence lattice is the wrong invariant}
\label{sec:congruence}

The first invariant one would try to transport along
$\Phi:S_A\to S_p$ is the lattice of congruences, the admissible
lumpings of the update dynamics.  It does transport, as an interval.
This section records that result and then shows that it is too weak
to carry the reading one would like: the interval is non-distributive
already for processes whose exact predictor stores one symbol, on
which every invariant of Section~\ref{sec:syntactic} is trivial.

\subsection{The inherited interval}

A congruence on a monoid $S$ is an equivalence relation
$\sim$ compatible with multiplication on both sides: $s\sim t$ implies
$asb\sim atb$ for all $a,b\in S$.  Compatibility is what makes
congruences the admissible lumpings of dynamics: it says that
substituting indistinguishable transformations into any past and future
dynamical context cannot make them distinguishable, so once
admissibility is defined as a lumping of transition operators, the
admissible hierarchy is exactly $\Con(S)$.\footnote{This is a
conditional statement: it does not make every algebraic congruence
meaningful for a particular agent's dynamics.}  This concerns
transformations, not predictive states, coarse graining states is a
different construction.

$\Con(S)$ denotes the set of all congruences on $S$, ordered by
refinement, the intersection of two congruences is a congruence, and any
family of congruences has a coarsest common refinement, so $\Con(S)$ is a
lattice, with bottom the equality relation and top the relation merging
everything.  The interval $[\kappa,\top]$ consists of the congruences at
least as coarse as $\kappa$, and the correspondence theorem says these are
in order-preserving bijection with the congruences of the quotient
$S/\kappa$.  Finally, $M_3$ and $N_5$ are the two five-element lattices shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:m3n5}, whose presence as a sublattice is equivalent to
the failure of distributivity (Section~\ref{sec:coordinates}).

\begin{corollary}[Inherited intervals]
\label{cor:con-inheritance}
Let $A$ be a reachable exact predictive agent for $p$, with
$\kappa=\ker\Phi$ and $\kappa'\supseteq\kappa$ the kernel of the
composite $S_A\to S_p\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$
(Corollaries~\ref{cor:quotient} and~\ref{cor:support-tower}).  Then
\[
  \Con(S_{\mathrm{supp}})
  \cong[\kappa',\top]
  \;\subseteq\;
  [\kappa,\top]
  \cong\Con(S_p)
  \;\subseteq\;
  \Con(S_A).
\]
In particular, if $\Con(S_p)$ is non-distributive then so is
$\Con(S_A)$: every $M_3$ or $N_5$ sublattice of $\Con(S_p)$ occurs
inside the interval $[\kappa,\top]$, since an interval is closed under
meets and joins.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The congruence correspondence theorem \cite{Howie1995}, applied to
the surjections $S_A\to S_p$ and $S_A\to S_{\mathrm{supp}}$.
\end{proof}

Different exact predictors may add implementation distinctions below
$\kappa$, but cannot remove the predictive interval above it.  We
call the non-distributive configurations the interval can carry
\emph{obstructions}.

\subsection{The reset chain: all three layers in closed form}
\label{sec:reset}

This subsection computes every layer of the tower for the simplest
nontrivial family of processes: full support first order Markov
chains. The possibility layer is
trivial (Remark~\ref{rem:full-support}), the update skeleton is a small
reset monoid whose congruence lattice is nevertheless non-distributive,
and the entire quantitative content, the actual probability values as
opposed to the update pattern, sits in the likelihood fibre.  In
particular, non distributivity of the inherited interval coexists with
a predictor that only stores the last symbol, so it cannot by itself
certify that internal features are entangled.

Let $\Obs=\{1,2,3\}$ and consider the stationary first-order Markov chain with
uniform stationary distribution (the matrix below is doubly stochastic)
and transition matrix
\[
  M=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \frac12&\frac13&\frac16\\[2pt]
    \frac13&\frac16&\frac12\\[2pt]
    \frac16&\frac12&\frac13
  \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The matrix is entrywise positive, and its three rows are distinct.  After
any nonempty history, the conditional future
law depend only on the last observed symbol, write $g_i$ for the
predictive state after observing $i$, its next symbol distribution is
row $i$ of $M$.  The chain is started in its stationary distribution,
(uniform because $M$ is doubly stochastic), so before the first
observation the initial predictive state $q_\eps$ assigns each symbol
probability $\tfrac13$.  No row of $M$ is uniform, so $q_\eps$ differs
from every $g_i$ already on the next symbol, and
\[
  X_p=\{q_\eps,g_1,g_2,g_3\}.
\]

Observing $i$ sends every state in $X_p$ to $g_i$ and fixes $\dagger$.  Let
$c_i$ denote this reset map.  The canonical predictive transition monoid is
\[
  R_3^1=\{1,c_1,c_2,c_3\},
  \qquad c_i c_j=c_i,
\]
where the displayed multiplication uses ordinary composition, with the left
factor applied last.  Figure~\ref{fig:reset} draws the action.

This example also makes
$\theta_{\mathrm{scal}}\subsetneq\kappa_{\mathrm{pred}}$ concrete.  The
restrictions of $g_1,g_2,g_3$ to one symbol futures are the rows of $M$, and
$\det M=-1/12$, so these states are linearly independent.  In that basis,
\[
  T_2g_i=M_{i2}g_2,
  \qquad
  T_{12}g_i=M_{i1}M_{12}g_2.
\]
The coefficient vectors are
\[
  \left(\frac13,\frac16,\frac12\right)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \left(\frac16,\frac19,\frac1{18}\right),
\]
which are not proportional.  Hence $T_2$ and $T_{12}$ are not scalar
multiples, although both reset every state in $X_p$ to $g_2$ and fix
$\dagger$.
In the language of Section~\ref{subsec:likelihood}, this shows two
distinct elements of the fibre of the likelihood extension over the
single reset $c_2$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:reset-con}
For $n\ge1$, let
\[
  R_n^1=\{1,c_1,\ldots,c_n\},
  \qquad c_i c_j=c_i,
\]
with $1$ an identity.  Every non-universal congruence has $\{1\}$ as a singleton
class and restricts to an arbitrary equivalence relation on
$\{c_1,\ldots,c_n\}$.  Consequently,
\[
  \Con(R_n^1)\cong \Pi_n\oplus\mathbf 1,
\]
the partition lattice on $n$ points with one new top element adjoined.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
First take any partition of the constants and keep $1$ in a single class.
It is a congruence.  Left multiplication of equivalent constants by $1$ preserves
their equivalence, left multiplication by a constant makes both products equal,
and the analogous right-multiplication checks follow from $c_ic_j=c_i$.

Conversely, suppose a congruence identifies $1$ with some $c_i$.  For every $j$,
right multiplication by $c_j$ gives
\[
  c_j=1c_j\equiv c_ic_j=c_i.
\]
Thus all constants are equivalent to $c_i$, and hence to $1$, the congruence is
universal.  Every non-universal congruence therefore fixes $1$, after which its
restriction to the constants is an arbitrary partition.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
For the three-symbol Markov chain above, $\Con(S_p)$ contains
\[
  \Pi_3\cong M_3
\]
as the interval below the congruence that merges all three reset maps while
leaving the identity separate.  In particular, $\Con(S_p)$ is
non distributive.


\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:markov-reset-general}
Let $p$ be a first order Markov chain on $n\ge2$ observation symbols whose
initial distribution is strictly positive and whose transition matrix is strictly positive and has pairwise distinct rows.  Then
\[
  S_p\cong R_n^1.
\]
Consequently,
\[ 
  \Con(S_p)\cong\Pi_n\oplus\mathbf 1,
\]
and it is non-distributive for every $n\ge3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
After every nonempty history, the conditional future law is the law $g_i$
associated with its last symbol $i$.  Distinct transition rows make the $g_i$
pairwise distinct.  Full support of both the initial distribution and the
transition matrix ensures that observing $i$ is possible from every live
predictive state and resets that state to $g_i$.  Thus the generated
transformations are precisely the identity and the $n$ distinct reset maps
$c_i$, which satisfy $c_ic_j=c_i$.  The congruence statement follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:reset-con}.
\end{proof}

The proposition does not ask that the initial state $q_\eps$ differ
from the $g_i$, as it does in the three-symbol chain above.  If
$q_\eps$ equals some $g_i$ then $X_p$ has $n$ states and not $n+1$,
and the monoid is the same.

An exact predictor for this process only needs to retain the latest symbol (and
an initial state before the first observation).  Its update rule is a reset, and
its predictions are the corresponding row of $M$ followed by the same Markov
law.  This representation is finite,
explicit, and transparent, Section~\ref{sec:rank} makes the word
transparent precise, as the statement that every generator has live
rank one, so the process licenses total forgetting at every step.
Nevertheless its canonical transition monoid has an $M_3$ congruence
interval.

Combining this example with Corollary~\ref{cor:con-inheritance} gives two
conclusions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item every reachable exact predictor of this process inherits a
        non distributive congruence interval,
  \item that non distributivity cannot itself imply that the predictor uses
        entangled, distributed, or uninterpretable features,
        Corollary~\ref{cor:projection} gives the mechanism, the witness
        lives in a projection that discards likelihoods, and here the
        likelihoods are the whole content.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{What distributivity does say}
\label{sec:coordinates}

In a finite
lattice $L$, an element $j\ne\bot$ is join irreducible when
$j=a\join b$ forces $j=a$ or $j=b$.  Every element of a finite lattice
is the join of the join irreducibles below it, so the assignment
\[
  x\longmapsto\J(x)=\{j\text{ join-irreducible}:j\le x\}
\]
is injective for \emph{every} finite lattice, not only a distributive
one.  Birkhoff's representation theorem for finite lattices says that
$L$ is distributive exactly when this assignment is a lattice
isomorphism onto the down-sets of the join-irreducibles (subsets
closed downwards, ordered by inclusion), equivalently, exactly when
$L$ contains neither $M_3$ nor $N_5$ of Figure~\ref{fig:m3n5} as a
sublattice \cite{DaveyPriestley2002}.

Reading $\J(x)$ as a bit-vector, the indicator of which
join-irreducibles lie below $x$, what fails without distributivity is
therefore not injectivity but the union rule
\[
  \J(a\join b)=\J(a)\cup\J(b).
\]
In $M_3$ the five elements still have five distinct bit-vectors, but
joining two atoms activates the third: the canonical coordinates are
not union-compositional, equivalently, there is no union/intersection
representation of the lattice.  This is the precise content of the
word obstruction as used in this paper: $M_3$ and $N_5$ block a
compositional coordinate representation of the lattice of admissible
lumpings, and nothing more is claimed for them.

Two cautions bound the reading from the other side.  Even
$\Con(S)\cong2^n$, the lattice of all subsets of an $n$-element set,
does not by itself prove that $S$ decomposes into independent
subsystems: a direct product decomposition requires complementary
factor congruences that permute,
$\alpha\circ\beta=\beta\circ\alpha$, where $\circ$ is composition of
relations, $a\,(\alpha\circ\beta)\,c$ meaning
$a\,\alpha\,b\,\beta\,c$ for some $b$
\cite[\S II.7]{BurrisSankappanavar1981}.  And the finite theorem is
applied in this paper only to explicitly finite monoids or explicitly
finite quotient intervals.

\subsection{Where the witnesses live}
\label{subsec:witnesses}

The computed examples of Section~\ref{subsec:computed-support} carry
witnesses too.  For Z1R, $|\Con(S_p)|=8$ with exactly one $M_3$
sublattice, and for RRXOR, $|\Con(S_p)|=29$, again with exactly one
$M_3$.  Since $\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}=\bot$ in both, every congruence
of $S_p$, the $M_3$ witness included, is an invariant of the support
language $L_p$ alone: any process with the same impossibility pattern
carries the same witness in its possibility interval.  The reset
chains are the opposite pole.  There $\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}=\top$,
and the $M_3$ contains $\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}$ in none of its five
elements except the top, so that witness is built entirely from
probabilities.  Because $\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}$ is trivial in Z1R and
RRXOR, these examples do not test whether a witness built from
impossibility always descends to the possibility interval.
Example~\ref{ex:one-zero} lies in the regime where the question is
open, and we have not computed its congruence lattices.

So the inherited interval is real, forced, and uninformative in the
sense that matters here.  It is non-distributive for every first
order Markov chain on three or more symbols, where the invariants of
Section~\ref{sec:syntactic} correctly report that nothing needs to be
composed: $R_n^1$ is aperiodic, has no nontrivial subgroup and group
complexity zero, and every generator has live rank one.

\section{Control}
\label{sec:controllers}

Everything above concerns prediction.  With actions, the alphabet
becomes $A\times O$ and the process becomes an interface
$\mathcal I(o_{1:n}\mid a_{1:n})$ whose outputs up to time $n$ depend
only on actions up to time $n$.  Its residuals after admissible
histories form a canonical machine $\widehat X_{\mathcal I}$ with
updates $\tau_{a,o}$ and monoid $S_{\mathcal I}$, the
$\varepsilon$-transducer construction \cite{BarnettCrutchfield2015}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:descent} and the absorbing dead state are all that
Sections~\ref{sec:agent}, \ref{sec:support}, \ref{sec:syntactic}
and~\ref{sec:rank} used, so each of their results holds verbatim for
any system with state set $Q$ and updates $\delta_{a,o}$ that admits
a surjection $L:Q\to\widehat X_{\mathcal I}$ with
$L\circ\delta_{a,o}=\tau_{a,o}\circ L$.

\begin{remark}[What a controller would need]
\label{rem:controllers}
For a predictor, exactness supplies the map $L$.  For a controller,
optimality does not.  If the only goal is the set of all streams,
every controller is optimal in every environment, including one with
a single live state, which cannot surject onto two predictive
states.  Richens et al.\ prove more: a policy optimal for every
myopic goal, a depth-one goal that must be met immediately, identifies no nontrivial bound on the transition
probabilities \cite{Richens2025}.  Near-optimality across a
sufficiently deep family of goals does determine the environment
from the policy \cite{Richens2025,Cifuentes2026}, but that recovery
reads the policy as a whole, not the controller's recurrent state.
The map $L$ exists when recovery is state-local, meaning that the
residual interface after a history is a function of the controller
state reached by that history, through the continuation policy from
that state.  Whether behavioural recovery yields state-locality in a
suitable exact limit is open, and we do not pursue it.  Under that
hypothesis a controller inherits the quotient onto $S_{\mathcal I}$,
and with it the transport and memory results of this paper.
\end{remark}

\section{An experiment: what trained transformers represent}
\label{sec:experiment}

The results above constrain the function a predictor computes.  They
do not say what a trained network stores.  This section reports one
experiment on two questions.  Does a transformer trained to predict
$p$ encode a stretch of input $w$ by its element $\tau_w$ of $S_p$,
or only by the state $\nu(uw)$ it leads to?  And does the algebraic
class of $S_p$ predict whether the trained predictor keeps working on
histories longer than those it was trained on?  Code and data are in
the repository \cite{DovetailRepo}.

\paragraph{Processes.}
Fix maps $g_0,g_1$ of $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ to itself.  The process
starts at the point $0$, and from the point $x$ it emits $0$ with
probability $\tfrac1{10}+\tfrac8{10}\cdot\tfrac{x}{n-1}$ and $1$
otherwise, then moves to $g_o(x)$ for the emitted symbol $o$.  Every
word is possible and the next-symbol laws of distinct points differ,
so when every point is reachable $X_p$ is the set of points and $S_p$
is the monoid generated by $g_0,g_1$.  We use six such processes,
chosen by their monoid, together with two standard ones, Z1R and
RRXOR \cite{ShaiEtAl2024}.  Table~\ref{tab:a2-lg} lists them; the
sizes are computed exactly.  The group cases all satisfy the
hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:pred-complexity}(ii), and $A_5$ that
of (iii).

One of the six separates two properties that otherwise coincide.  The
clamped walk and Z1R are aperiodic and also have synchronising words,
after which the state is known whatever came before; the group
processes have neither property.  An aperiodic process with no
synchronising word cannot be strongly connected.  Indeed, let $M$ be
a finite aperiodic transformation monoid in which every point can be
moved to every other, and let $e$ be an idempotent of minimum rank.
Then $eMe=\{e\}$, because it is a group in an aperiodic monoid.  For
$y,y'$ in the image of $e$ choose $m$ with $m(y)=y'$; then
$y=e(y)=eme(y)=e(y')=y'$, so $e$ is constant.  The \emph{latched}
process therefore has a transient part: on six points,
$g_0=(1,2,2,2,4,4)$ and $g_1=(0,4,3,3,5,5)$ as lists of images.  The
symbol after the first $0$ selects one of the components $\{2,3\}$
and $\{4,5\}$, and inside a component the state is the last symbol.
Its monoid has $15$ elements, is aperiodic, and has minimum rank $2$:
no word erases which component was chosen.

\paragraph{Training.}
A causal transformer reads a start token followed by sampled symbols
and is trained at every position to predict the next symbol.
Training sequences have $16$ symbols and are sampled afresh at every
step.  Positions are learned embeddings, shifted by a random offset
during training so that every position up to $96$ is trained.  Depths
are $1,2,4,6$ with widths $128,92,64,52$, which keeps the parameter
count between $2.0\times10^5$ and $2.1\times10^5$.  There are four
seeds for each process and depth, $128$ models in all, each trained
for $30{,}000$ steps; a further $32$ models trained on $64$ symbols
are described below.  The loss we report is the exact
Kullback--Leibler divergence from $n_p(u)$ to the model's prediction,
averaged over sampled histories $u$, divided by the same divergence
for the best predictor that ignores the history.  A value of $0$ is
exact prediction and $1$ is no better than ignoring the input.

\paragraph{Length generalisation.}
Table~\ref{tab:a2-lg} and Figure~\ref{fig:a2-lg} give the loss at
positions $1$ to $16$, which were trained, and at $17$ to $32$ and
$33$ to $64$, which were not.  Every model predicts well at trained
lengths, and deeper models predict better.  Beyond the trained
length the processes split by whether $S_p$ is aperiodic.  At
positions $17$ to $32$, $44$ of the $48$ models of the three
aperiodic processes have loss below $0.5$, with median $0.013$, and
the four exceptions are the latched process at depth $1$; of the $64$
models of the four group processes $25$ do, with median $0.57$ and
minimum $0.28$ (Mann--Whitney $p<10^{-14}$).  At positions $33$ to
$64$ no group model is below $0.73$ and the median is $1.08$: at four
times the trained length the group models are no better than ignoring
the input.  RRXOR, whose monoid contains $\mathbb Z_3$, behaves like
the groups.

The latched process says which property this is.  It has no
synchronising word, and from depth $2$ it generalises better than
either process that has one: all twelve models are below $0.1$ at
positions $33$ to $64$, and eight are below $0.01$.  So a reset word
is not what the aperiodic models rely on.  At depth $1$ the latched
models fail beyond the trained length, with loss $0.75$ at positions
$17$ to $32$, the only aperiodic case that does.

Two predictions one might draw from Table~\ref{tab:classes} are not
borne out.  First, the solvable groups do not behave differently
from $A_5$.  At depths $2$, $4$ and $6$ the losses of the $A_5$
models and of the models of the three solvable groups are not
distinguishable at either unseen range (Mann--Whitney $p>0.2$ in all
six comparisons; at depth $1$ and positions $17$ to $32$ the $A_5$
models are worse, $p=0.01$).  Second, depth does not buy length.
Depth lowers the loss of the group models at positions $17$ to $32$,
from about $0.9$ at depth $1$ to about $0.4$ at depth $6$, but not at
positions $33$ to $64$.  Neither observation contradicts
Corollary~\ref{cor:transformers}, which is asymptotic and concerns
what can be expressed, not what training finds; at these lengths
every one of these processes can be predicted exactly at these
depths \cite{LiuEtAl2023}.  The line that training respects here is
the one between $\mathrm{AC}^0$ and its complement, not the one
between solvable and non-solvable groups.

A longer trained length does not change this.  We retrained the
clamped walk, $S_3$, $S_4$ and $A_5$ at depths $2$ and $6$ on
histories of $64$ symbols, four seeds each, and evaluated to $256$
(Table~\ref{tab:a2-long}).  At positions $65$ to $128$ every model of
the clamped walk is below $0.19$ and every group model is above
$0.37$; at positions $129$ to $256$ the group models are again no
better than ignoring the input.  No gap opens between $A_5$ and the
solvable groups: beyond the trained length they are not
distinguishable at either depth (Mann--Whitney $p>0.4$ in all four
comparisons).  Within the trained length the loss rises with the
size of the group, from $0.02$ for $S_3$ to $0.07$ for $A_5$ at depth
$2$, which is an ordering by size and not by solvability.  The
clamped walk also degrades at four times this trained length, to
$0.34$ at depth $6$.

\paragraph{Element or state.}
For the last $m$ symbols $w$ of a history ($m=6$, and $m=8$ for $A_5$
and RRXOR) we fit a linear classifier from the residual stream at the
end of $w$ to the element $\tau_w$, on half of $3{,}000$ fresh
sequences of the trained length, and score it on the other half,
taking the best layer.  Two comparisons say what the score means.
The first is the best guess of $\tau_w$ from the predictive states
before and after $w$, which is what a network that tracks only the
state could support.  The second is the same classifier fitted to a
random regrouping of the words into classes of the same sizes, which
is what remembering the literal symbols supports.  A second,
\emph{counterfactual} readout asks for $\tau_w(y)$ at states $y$ the
process was not in before $w$, with the same two comparisons; only a
representation of the transformation can support it.

The element readout never beats both comparisons by more than
$0.02$ at depths $4$ and $6$, in any process or seed
(Table~\ref{tab:a2-probe}).  For the aperiodic processes the element
is almost determined by the two states, and the readout matches that
ceiling.  For the groups it falls below the state-only ceiling as
depth grows.  For $S_3$, $S_4$ and $A_5$ it stays within a few points
of the regrouping control, so what is readable of the element is
what is readable of the symbols.  The counterfactual readout does
somewhat better in the shallow group models.  It is clearest for
$A_5$ at depth $2$, where it is $0.65$ against $0.52$ for both
comparisons, with a margin of at least $0.11$ in all four seeds; the
margin is about $0.05$ at depth $4$ and at most $0.04$ at depth $6$.
Probing the saved checkpoints from step $500$ to the end gives the
same picture at every stage: for the group processes at depths $4$
and $6$ the element readout is below the comparisons at every
checkpoint, so the element is not built early and then discarded,
and the $A_5$ margin at depth $2$ grows steadily through training.

What the deeper networks do contain is the state, and nearly nothing
else.  At depth $6$ the predictive state is linearly readable with
accuracy $0.94$ to $1.00$ in the six constructed processes, and
the share of residual variance not explained by the state is at most
$7\%$.  Clustering the final residuals into $|X_p|$
clusters and reading off the map from a cluster and a symbol to the
next cluster gives, for $A_5$, $S_3$ and
$\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3$ at depth $6$, a deterministic
automaton whose clusters are single predictive states and whose
transition monoid has exactly $|S_p|$ elements, in every seed.  So
$S_p$ is present in these networks in the sense the theory requires,
as the monoid of the update that the computed function induces on
states, and not as a represented quantity.  We did not run
interventions on an element subspace, because the readouts found no
such subspace to intervene on.

\paragraph{Limits.}
The networks are small, and the longest trained length is $64$.  The separation of aperiodicity from
synchronisation rests on one process, and in it what no word erases
is a single bit fixed near the start of the sequence; a transient
part of this kind is forced by the argument above, but larger or
later-set memories of the kind were not tested.  The readouts are linear, and a non-linear code for $\tau_w$
would not be found.  For $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3$ the element
is a function of the two states, so that row does not separate the
hypotheses.  And the experiment concerns predictors that are
approximate and that fail off the trained lengths, which is outside
the hypotheses of every theorem in this paper.

\section{Results and limitations}
\label{sec:results}

The theorem chain is short.  Conditional future laws form a canonical
machine, every reachable exact predictor surjects onto it, and
descent makes the canonical monoid $S_p$ a quotient of the
predictor's update monoid.  That monoid is the syntactic monoid of
the state labelling.  For finite predictors its subgroups, its
aperiodicity and its group complexity are forced, with the bounds
attained.  For predictors with no recurrent state the same algebra
fixes the circuit class of exact prediction.  In a small experiment,
transformers trained on short histories kept predicting on longer
ones only when $S_p$ was aperiodic, with no difference between
solvable and non-solvable groups.  The rank profile bounds
forced memory word by word, and subgroups sit at rank levels that
bound them.  Around $S_p$ sit a possibility quotient and a likelihood
extension that none of these invariants see, and the reset chains and
the computed examples bracket where a process's structure can live:
entirely in the likelihoods, or entirely in bare possibility.  The
congruence lattice is inherited too, and is uninformative.

Four limits should be read alongside.  First, nothing here locates
features in a network's activations: the results constrain update
dynamics and the functions computed, not representations.  In the one
experiment we ran (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}), small trained
transformers encode the current state and not the element of $S_p$
of a stretch of input.
Second, the transport theorem needs a finite update monoid
(Example~\ref{ex:free-predictor}), and the statement for predictors
without one is conditional on $\mathrm{TC}^0\ne\mathrm{NC}^1$ and
asymptotic in the length.  Third, accuracy must be uniform over
possible histories (Remark~\ref{rem:uniform}).  Fourth, the
individual objects are close to known territory, causal states,
follower-set automata, syntactic monoids, synchronisation theory, the
projective/likelihood split, and the claims made here are the
passage from a process to its monoid and the transport of that
structure to arbitrary exact predictors, not the objects themselves.

Four problems are left open.  An average-case version: for a
finite-state process whose observer synchronises asymptotically
\cite{TraversCrutchfield2011b}, a predictor reading a bounded window
can be accurate on most histories without composing anything, so we
expect that the algebra is not forced on such predictors as stated,
and we do not know the right weakening.  Whether an impossibility-built congruence
witness always descends to the possibility layer, testable in the
regime $\bot\subsetneq\kappa_{\mathrm{supp}}\subsetneq\top$ of
Example~\ref{ex:one-zero} (Section~\ref{subsec:witnesses}).  A
criterion separating congruence obstructions that block genuine
decompositions of the monoid from those that are counting artefacts,
congruence permutability is the natural candidate
(Section~\ref{sec:coordinates}).  And, for controllers,
state-locality (Section~\ref{sec:controllers}).
